ExoSett Reconfigurability

From Building Architecture to Interchangeable Components

ExoSett is an architecture for reconfigurable buildings. Independently supported accommodation pods can be installed, withdrawn and replaced without compromising the primary structural support. The architecture is designed to keep neighbouring homes occupied and largely undisturbed during these changes. Interruptions should be the exception.

Physical architecture alone is insufficient. Components made by different manufacturers, potentially decades apart, must conform to compatible interfaces. Three complementary elements connect the architectural principle to dependable interchangeability: the architecture, the digital building specification and the physical jig.

1. The architecture: making change possible

The accommodation frame provides exoskeletal structural support for the accommodation pods. Each pod is supported independently of its neighbours. The service frame provides the associated exoskeletal framework for circulation and services. Separating these responsibilities allows accommodation to change while the supporting structure and shared routes remain in place.

In conventional stacked modular construction, modules commonly transmit loads from those above. Removing a lower module can therefore affect the support of others. ExoSett separates the accommodation from that primary load path.

Independent support must be accompanied by planned insertion and removal routes, suitable handling arrangements and accessible connections. Together, these provisions allow reconfiguration throughout the building’s life. The Components pages, including the Frame node, explain the individual parts and their interfaces.

2. The digital specification: defining what must fit

The building specification provides a common definition of the building’s arrangement and requirements for designers, manufacturers, installers and maintainers. Its XML design document is a machine-readable project record, independent of any particular design application.

The existing XML building model describes frame pairs, their cells, abstract pod types and pod placements, with dimensions and relative positions. It does not yet define the full engineering requirements for manufacture, assembly or operation. The broader building specification must establish or reference component definitions, physical and services interfaces, tolerances, performance requirements and verification procedures. Checking XML against its schema establishes document structure, not engineering conformity.

The services designer must work with the building designer, frame designer and pod designer to ensure that services interfaces are defined in the design document. Connections, isolation, access and the procedures for disconnecting and reconnecting a pod are part of making replacement practical while other homes remain in use.

Each project’s XML design document should be under version control, such as Git, from the outset. Its revision history records design decisions and subsequent changes. This history is orthogonal to the schema version: the schema defines the document format, while a project revision identifies a particular state of the design. Many design revisions may use the same schema version, and changing schema is a deliberate decision. The modelling documentation explains this versioning policy.

Retaining the applicable design revisions and referenced definitions supports manufacture of replacement components long after construction. Records must continue to identify the requirements that apply to the building as maintained.

Compatibility is specification-specific. Many buildings may share the same pod and interface requirements while differing in other respects, such as their foundations. A possible future schema extension would allow design documents to import common specification parts, sharing the bulk of a specification while retaining building-specific provisions. This is a proposed capability, rather than a feature of the current XML format.

3. The physical jig: establishing conformity

The physical jig embodies the critical interfaces for a specified design. Its definition belongs in the design document, together with the applicable tolerances. It may embody connection positions, dimensional boundaries, insertion clearances and other essential geometry. The building designer owns the coordinated jig definition.

A newly manufactured jig must first be verified by measurement against that definition and its tolerances. The verified jig can then assist manufacture and test components against the specified interfaces before they arrive on site. It provides a common physical reference for both pods and accommodation frames.

Only pods that conform to the applicable specification and pass the prescribed jig tests may be installed. This requirement applies wherever that specification is used; it does not restrict a conforming pod to a single building.

Manufacturers may use different materials, production methods and equipment, provided their components satisfy the relevant requirements. Equivalent jigs can be produced from the same specification and verified by measurement, allowing geographically distributed and competing manufacturers to work against the same interfaces.

A successful jig test establishes conformity only for the requirements that test covers. It does not replace structural calculations, fire testing, material certification or other necessary verification.

4. Reconfigurability throughout the building’s life

The architecture provides independent support and routes for change. The specification records what components must satisfy. The verified jig gives manufacturers and installers a practical means of checking the critical physical interfaces. Together, they support independent manufacture and reliable assembly, followed by maintenance, replacement and reconfiguration as requirements change.

A withdrawn pod can be refurbished for further use. Reuse in another building depends on conformity to that building’s applicable requirements, including the relevant jig tests. Maintaining specifications and conformity records allows this decision to be made from evidence.

Long-term evolution must preserve the relevant structural, safety and operational requirements. A revised arrangement, replacement pod or changed service connection must be assessed against those requirements, with the design record updated accordingly. Planning isolation, access and handling is essential to keeping neighbouring homes largely undisturbed.

5. An architecture for independent participation

ExoSett aims to allow multiple independent manufacturers to compete to supply conforming components. Rather than making future change depend on one manufacturer’s product range, designers should be able to specify what is required without unnecessarily prescribing how every component is made.

This independence supports competition, resilience of supply and long-term availability of replacements. It depends on sufficiently complete specifications, controlled interfaces and credible verification procedures. It does not imply that every ExoSett pod fits every ExoSett building, or that there is one universal ExoSett engineering specification.

The XML modelling format already exists. Complete manufacturing interface standards, physical jig definitions and certification procedures are still being developed. Interchangeability across independent manufacturers has not yet been demonstrated. These are the intended means of delivering the architecture’s reconfigurability in a manufactured system. The intended licensing model does not, by itself, settle whether future technical standards will be open or proprietary.

Reconfigurability as an engineered property

The architecture makes reconfiguration possible. The specification defines it. The jig helps make it dependable.

Reconfigurability is an engineered property of the complete ExoSett system. Structural separation enables change; coordinated specification and verified conformity make that change dependable throughout the building’s life.
