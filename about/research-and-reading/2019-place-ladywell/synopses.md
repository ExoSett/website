## Synopsis

The Royal Holloway report investigates how families experienced PLACE/Ladywell, a Lewisham Council development of modular, relocatable temporary accommodation designed with Rogers Stirk Harbour + Partners. Its 24 homes were widely praised for architectural and technical innovation, but the researchers identify a gap between professional celebration and knowledge of everyday life inside the scheme. Drawing on in-depth interviews conducted with residents between April and September 2017, the report asks what difference the development made, how residents arrived there, what they valued or disliked, and whether it changed their sense of security and future prospects.

PLACE/Ladywell offered important improvements over much temporary accommodation residents had previously experienced. Families valued self-contained, comparatively spacious and well-designed dwellings, greater privacy and a location within their borough. Remaining near schools, work, healthcare, family and social networks mattered greatly, especially after earlier experiences or fears of out-of-borough placement. The physical home could provide relief, stability in daily routines and a better setting for family life.

Those improvements did not make the housing permanent. Residents remained homeless in the administrative and experiential sense, waiting for secure long-term housing and uncertain when or where they would move. The project’s planned mobility and temporary tenancies could reinforce awareness that occupation was provisional. Families worried about further disruption to children’s education, employment, care, friendships and support networks. The report therefore distinguishes the quality of a dwelling from the security and continuity of a housing situation.

Home-making emerged as a significant issue. Management rules and concern for the units as reusable assets constrained some ordinary personalisation, such as attaching decorations or making small changes. Residents’ accounts of fixtures, finishes and domestic practices show that feeling at home depends partly on the ability to occupy, alter and mark a space as one’s own. Treating a dwelling primarily as a mobile product can conflict with its role as somebody’s home.

The development’s visual identity generated ambivalence. Its distinctive appearance and publicity made some residents feel conspicuous or labelled as homeless. Architectural innovation that attracts awards and media attention can also expose occupants to observation and stigma. The report asks project promoters to consider how language, appearance, visitor attention and management affect residents rather than assuming positive professional reception translates into a positive domestic experience.

The authors’ “sticking plaster” conclusion is carefully scoped. PLACE/Ladywell can materially improve temporary accommodation and offers lessons for future schemes, but it cannot repair the structural shortage of secure, affordable housing. A good temporary home may improve the period of waiting; it does not remove uncertainty or provide permanent progress unless families can move into suitable secure housing. The news article summarising the research emphasises this distinction and reports recommendations to give residents greater home-making freedom, minimise stigma and keep their experiences central to evaluation.

The research does not reject modular construction. Instead, it challenges claims that mobility, speed or design quality alone constitute a solution to homelessness. Its main contribution is to place residents’ accounts beside institutional and architectural narratives and to show that permanence is relational and temporal: continuity of school, work, family, neighbourhood and control over domestic space matters alongside the physical standard of the unit.

## Main arguments and findings

- PLACE/Ladywell improved physical conditions and quality of life compared with some prior temporary accommodation.
- Remaining in-borough helped preserve access to schools, employment, healthcare and social networks.
- Temporary tenure and uncertainty about the next move continued to create anxiety and insecurity.
- Good design cannot substitute for adequate, affordable permanent housing.
- Restrictions on decoration and alteration could inhibit ordinary home-making.
- Distinctive design, publicity and labels could make residents feel visible or stigmatised.
- Resident experience should be part of assessing and designing future temporary-accommodation projects.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The study concerns one unusual, highly publicised development and a limited group of interviewed residents.
- Interviews capture experience during a particular stage of occupation; long-term trajectories were not yet known.
- Findings about management, visibility and home-making may not transfer unchanged to other modular schemes.
- The report compares residents’ accounts of previous accommodation rather than using a matched control group.
- Positive and negative effects arise from tenure, location, design, management and wider housing scarcity together, not from modular construction alone.
- The short Royal Holloway news article is a summary; this synopsis relies primarily on the underlying report record and report.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following is ExoSett’s interpretation.

- The research supports a distinction between physically changeable buildings and insecure housing: movable components need not imply temporary tenure.
- It exposes a risk that designing modules for later reuse could lead managers to restrict ordinary occupation and personalisation.
- ExoSett’s refurbishment concept could absorb normal wear between deployments, but resident rights and control during occupation need explicit treatment.
- Long-lived frames and services might allow accommodation to change while preserving location and networks; this is a hypothesis, not a result of the report.
- Architectural identity should respond to place and residents rather than advertise construction method or tenure.
- Further research is required on disruption during reconfiguration, consent, continuity of tenure, domestic alteration and how benefits of reuse are shared.
