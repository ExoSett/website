Modular housing and permanence

Research with residents of PLACE/Ladywell separates improvements in temporary accommodation from the security and continuity of a permanent home.

Royal Holloway’s September 2019 article, “Pop-up” modular housing solution is no more than a sticking plaster, research finds, reports independent research by Ella Harris, Katherine Brickell and Mel Nowicki. This ExoSett discussion does not imply endorsement by Royal Holloway or the researchers.

Better temporary housing

Physical quality does not provide security and continuity

The research found that PLACE/Ladywell improved on some other forms of temporary accommodation. Residents nevertheless remained uncertain about what would happen next and anxious about further moves.

A well-designed temporary dwelling cannot remove the disruption associated with uncertain future housing, repeated relocation, changing schools, losing proximity to work or healthcare, or separation from friends, neighbours and support networks. ExoSett agrees with the underlying criticism: modular temporary accommodation is not a substitute for adequate permanent housing, and no building system can solve housing policy or homelessness by itself.

Permanent system, flexible accommodation

Permanence of place need not freeze the building

ExoSett offers a permanent housing system with accommodation that can change.

In social housing, the site, relationship with the neighbourhood, accommodation frame, service frame, circulation, communal areas and much utility infrastructure might persist for decades. Individual modules, dwelling configurations, layouts, fittings, finishes and services supplied to accommodation might change.

ExoSett therefore seeks permanence of place and infrastructure without requiring permanence of accommodation configuration. This is a design ambition, not a demonstrated outcome.

Change within the building

Ask whether accommodation could change before requiring a resident to move

Conventional housing often responds to changed household needs through relocation. An adaptable building may sometimes respond by changing accommodation: providing more or less space, addressing new accessibility or care requirements, or responding as children arrive or leave and households change.

Not every change can or should happen without a move. The principle is that relocation need not automatically be the only architectural response. ExoSett seeks to place adaptability in the building system rather than continually asking residents to adapt to unsuitable accommodation. Persistent frames, services and interfaces could provide a stable platform for change, although this has not yet been proven in constructed ExoSett buildings.

Continuity of place

A good location becomes more valuable with time

The research highlights residents’ concerns about remaining close to schools, employment, family, friends and support networks. A location is more than nearby amenities: its value also accumulates through people’s relationships with a place.

Long-lived ExoSett infrastructure may allow housing to change while preserving some continuity. This is a conceptual possibility, not a claim that physical adaptability guarantees social continuity. The Location page develops this design consideration.

A home must be lived in

The cassette concept must not govern domestic life

Some PLACE/Ladywell residents reported that restrictions on small decorative changes made it harder to feel at home. Homes are not display units. Decorating, hanging pictures or shelves, moving furniture, wear, minor damage, repairs and personalisation are ordinary parts of occupation.

During manufacture, transport, installation, removal and refurbishment, it may be useful to think of an accommodation module as a cassette. During occupation, it is somebody’s home. A housing system should not ask residents to preserve accommodation as transport equipment simply because it may later be removed or reused.

Reasonable domestic use should be expected and designed for, while structural, fire-safety, service and tenancy constraints still apply. The Accommodation design page sets out this principle.

Reuse and refurbishment

Design for occupation, then design for renewal

A realistic intended lifecycle is manufacture → installation → occupation → removal → inspection, refurbishment or adaptation → subsequent installation. Reuse should not mean passing somebody else’s worn home unchanged to the next resident.

Between deployments, an appropriate facility might repair damage, renew worn surfaces and finishes, inspect the structure, check or replace services, update equipment, alter internal arrangements where practical, or adapt the module for another household or use. This is a design ambition, not a developed ExoSett refurbishment standard or demonstrated commercial process.

If renewal is expected, residents need not be treated as custodians of a pristine product. Normal home-making can be accommodated during occupation, with its effects addressed later through repair and refurbishment.

Architectural identity and stigma

The construction system need not be the building’s public identity

PLACE/Ladywell residents also described feeling conspicuous or marked out by aspects of the development’s appearance. As the FEANTSA discussion of modular housing without second-class housing argues, people should not be identifiable or stigmatised by the construction method or tenure of their home.

Façades, balconies, entrances, landscaping, roofs and materials can respond to context while adaptable engineering sits behind them. The construction system need not determine architectural identity.

Permanence without immobility

The housing offer can persist while components change

Permanent housing does not require every physical component to remain unchanged. A long-lived system may maintain continuity while components are repaired, upgraded, replaced, reconfigured or refurbished. Maintainability can contribute to usefulness without promising indefinite life.

Physical change inside a building does not by itself make the housing temporary. The important distinction is between insecurity for residents and planned change in the infrastructure that supports their homes.

What ExoSett takes from this

Flexibility should support permanence

Better temporary accommodation cannot provide the continuity of adequate permanent housing. ExoSett is not interesting simply because accommodation can move; its stronger possibility is that a permanent place may change around the lives of the people who live there.

The aim is not temporary housing made easier to move, but permanent housing made easier to change.
