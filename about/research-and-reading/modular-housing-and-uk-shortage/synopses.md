# An Exploration of the Potential for Using Modular Housing Solutions to Address the UK’s Housing Shortage

## Source

- Slug: `modular-housing-and-uk-shortage`
- Authors / organisation: Joseph Kwame Ofori-Kuragu and Reece Hill; Proceedings of the CIB International Conference on Smart Built Environment (ICSBE 2021)
- Publication date: December 2021
- Original URL: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358711883_An_Exploration_of_the_Potential_for_Using_Modular_Housing_Solutions_to_Address_the_UK%27s_Housing_Shortage
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

This conference paper examines whether modular housing could contribute to addressing the UK’s shortage of affordable homes and construction skills. It combines a literature review with an online questionnaire of construction-industry participants. The authors frame high house-price-to-income ratios, inadequate housing supply and a shortage of skilled labour as linked pressures that conventional housebuilding has struggled to resolve.

The literature review describes modular construction as shifting substantial work into controlled factory conditions, with completed or substantially completed units transported to site. Reported advantages include predictable production, speed, reduced site labour, less material waste, quality control, lower maintenance and potential cost savings. Factory activity may use a differently organised workforce and reduce dependence on scarce on-site trades. The review also connects design for manufacture and assembly with digital coordination and Building Information Modelling.

The paper does not present modular construction as free of constraints. It notes high initial capital requirements, including factory investment and payment before units arrive on site; transport restrictions; limited materials and layouts associated with mass production; and difficulty accommodating late design changes. Standardisation can create efficiency but reduce customisation. Traditional construction was perceived as having a longer lifespan. The review therefore provides both favourable claims and reasons that adoption has remained limited.

The survey was administered online during COVID-19 restrictions. Seventy structured questionnaires were sent, although the paper’s rendered text does not make the achieved response count as prominent as the distribution figure. Respondents were mainly construction professionals, with commercial organisations and main contractors strongly represented. Their answers measure beliefs and awareness rather than completed-project performance.

Forty-five per cent agreed or strongly agreed that modular construction had good potential as an alternative to conventional methods, while 30% disagreed and 25% were unsure. Half agreed that it could become a mainstream future method and 18% disagreed. When respondents selected potential benefits, speed was the most common response (29%), followed by environmentally preferable materials (25%), waste reduction (20%), lower transport emissions (16%) and thermal insulation (10%). The authors interpret the distribution, together with uncertainty and disagreement, as evidence that industry awareness and confidence remain limited.

The paper concludes that modular housing has potential to deliver homes faster and more cheaply while reducing waste and construction-related emissions. It also associates reduced maintenance with lower life-cycle cost, while acknowledging the perceived longevity advantage of traditional housing. The authors recommend education about modular benefits and further research into the cost of contractors establishing manufacturing facilities. They also propose studying closer integration of value engineering and BIM. Because planned follow-up interviews could not be undertaken during the pandemic, the analysis lacks the qualitative confirmation originally desired.

Overall, the paper is best read as a record of literature claims and industry expectations at a particular time. It argues that modular construction could contribute to supply and skills problems, but it does not quantify the share of the housing shortage it could meet or empirically verify comparative cost, speed, carbon, quality or lifespan.

## Main arguments and findings

- Housing affordability and skilled-labour shortages create a case for alternative production methods.
- Literature associates modular housing with speed, factory quality control, lower waste, reduced on-site labour and possible cost benefits.
- Survey respondents most often identified speed, materials, waste, transport emissions and insulation as potential benefits.
- Respondents were divided about modular construction’s potential and mainstream adoption, indicating substantial uncertainty.
- Barriers include capital and factory costs, transport, limited customisation, late-change difficulty and perceived shorter life.
- The authors recommend awareness-building and further work on manufacturing costs, value engineering and BIM.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The survey records perceptions, not measured performance of modular projects.
- The sample is relatively small and concentrated among industry stakeholders, especially contractors and commercial organisations.
- The paper gives the number of questionnaires distributed; care is needed not to assume all 70 produced usable responses.
- COVID-19 restrictions prevented planned confirmatory interviews.
- Literature claims come from sources of varied type and age and are not independently tested in the paper.
- Cost, carbon, programme and lifespan claims lack a consistent comparative basis.
- Housing shortage and affordability have land, finance, planning and policy causes that manufacturing alone cannot resolve.
