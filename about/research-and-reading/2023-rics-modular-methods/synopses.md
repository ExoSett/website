# How modular methods can redress housing crisis

## Source

- Slug: `2023-rics-modular-methods`
- Author / organisation: Dr James L. Broun; *RICS Property Journal*; author identified as policy adviser at Make UK Modular
- Publication date: 15 September 2023
- Original URL: https://ww3.rics.org/uk/en/journals/property-journal/mmc-modular-homes-housing-supply.html
- Date accessed: 22 August 2026

## Synopsis

This *RICS Property Journal* article makes a property-industry case for modern methods of construction (MMC), especially factory-built modular housing, as a response to constraints on UK housebuilding. It links inadequate housing supply and worsening affordability to labour and skills shortages, low productivity, limited innovation and the growing technical demands of net-zero regulation. Planning adds cost, complexity and uncertainty, the article argues, but is not the sole or principal explanation for undersupply because many consented homes remain unbuilt and the industry lacks the workforce to scale conventional construction substantially.

The article describes an ageing construction workforce, weak apprenticeship replacement and reduced access to European labour. It also characterises building construction as less productive than it was in the 1990s and construction R&D spending as low. At the same time, new operational-carbon standards, the prospect of embodied-carbon regulation, retrofit demand and the skills required by new materials increase pressure on the existing labour model.

MMC is presented as a way to bypass some of those constraints. The article distinguishes panellised or two-dimensional systems from volumetric or three-dimensional construction. In the latter, floors, walls, ceilings and roofs are combined around a steel or timber frame in a factory to create substantial portions of a house or apartment, then transported and lifted into place.

Drawing principally on Make UK Modular research and member data, the article reports that modular houses may require less than half the workforce of conventional housebuilding, that modular manufacture may be up to 40% more productive, and that only a minority of the factory workforce may need traditional trades. It also argues that manufacturing roles can lower some barriers to entry and attract a more diverse workforce. The claimed delivery advantages include completion up to 50% faster, concurrent factory and site work, less snagging, reduced maintenance and borrowing costs, and greater investment in R&D.

The article connects controlled factory conditions with environmental performance. It argues that factories make airtightness, waste reduction, embodied-carbon monitoring and preparation for future standards easier. It cites examples and early programme evidence suggesting modular homes can achieve strong operational- and embodied-carbon performance without prohibitive cost uplifts.

Commercial difficulties are acknowledged. Two major modular producers had recently closed, citing inflation, reduced demand and challenging financial markets, and client confidence had weakened. The author nevertheless argues that the sector is diverse, spanning manufacturers of different scales, methods and materials, and notes that modular construction is already established in commercial, education and healthcare markets. Accreditation, warranties, fire testing and factory quality-control processes are presented as responses to safety and longevity concerns.

The conclusion is strongly favourable: the underlying supply, labour, productivity, speed and environmental pressures will persist, so surveyors and others involved in development should understand modular methods. The article does not advocate replacing all traditional construction and notes that small sites and complex off-plan projects may be unsuitable.

## Main arguments and findings

- UK housing supply is constrained not only by planning but by shortages of labour and skills, weak productivity and limited innovation.
- Net-zero requirements add technical, workforce and cost pressures to conventional housebuilding.
- Factory manufacture can reorganise work, reduce reliance on traditional trades and support more repeatable quality control.
- The article reports potential gains in productivity, delivery speed, environmental performance, snagging and workforce diversity.
- Modular methods are not suitable for every site or project and are presented as additional capacity rather than a universal replacement for conventional building.
- Recent closures of major modular manufacturers damaged confidence, although the author argues that the wider and more diverse sector can endure.

## Qualifications and limitations

- This is an advocacy-oriented journal article written by a policy adviser at Make UK Modular, not an independent systematic review or comparative project evaluation.
- Several quantitative benefit claims come from Make UK Modular research or member data; the article does not provide a common project-level methodology for verifying them.
- Examples of carbon and cost performance do not establish typical sector-wide outcomes.
- The discussion of producer failures is brief and attributes them principally to macroeconomic and market conditions rather than examining business models, procurement pipelines or project-specific causes in depth.
- Housing supply and affordability also depend on land, planning, finance, tenure, infrastructure and demand, which manufacturing methods cannot resolve alone.
- The article describes the market and policy context in September 2023; subsequent events are outside its scope.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following is ExoSett’s interpretation. The article does not discuss or endorse ExoSett.

- The labour, productivity, quality-control, delivery and net-zero pressures provide mainstream property-industry context for ExoSett’s interest in factory-produced accommodation.
- Conventional volumetric construction generally makes three-dimensional portions of the final building. ExoSett proposes that accommodation modules remain independently replaceable within a persistent exoskeletal accommodation frame.
- Modular manufacture addresses how accommodation can be produced efficiently. ExoSett asks a further question: if factory-produced accommodation modules are advantageous, why should their relationship with the building necessarily be permanent?
- Separating structure, services and accommodation could allow each to be maintained, replaced or adapted on a different timescale, enabling buildings to evolve after initial construction. This remains an ExoSett design proposition rather than a finding supported by the article.
- The manufacturer closures highlight the risk of depending on a single vertically integrated supplier. In principle, stable interfaces could support an ecosystem of compatible modules from multiple manufacturers over time, but interoperability, certification, governance and market viability would all need to be demonstrated.
- The article is useful background reading; its evidence and claims cannot be transferred directly to ExoSett’s system or used to claim that ExoSett already delivers the stated benefits.
