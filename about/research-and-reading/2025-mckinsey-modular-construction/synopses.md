# Putting the pieces together: Unlocking success in modular construction

## Source

- Slug: `2025-mckinsey-modular-construction`
- Authors / organisation: Erik Sjödin and Shankar Chandrasekaran, with Dave Dauphinais, Erlend Spets and Omar Kaakani; McKinsey & Company
- Publication date: 21 August 2025
- Original URL: https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/engineering-construction-and-building-materials/our-insights/putting-the-pieces-together-unlocking-success-in-modular-construction
- Date accessed: 22 August 2026

## Synopsis

McKinsey examines the characteristics associated with successful modular-construction businesses. Its perspective combines a proprietary database of more than 700 companies operating across more than 50 countries with interviews with more than 20 industry executives. McKinsey estimates that the database covers roughly 40 to 50 percent of the market and includes specialist 2D and 3D modular businesses as well as conventional construction companies active in modular construction.

The article defines modular construction as off-site manufacture followed by on-site assembly and includes 3D volumetric modules, 2D panels and bathroom pods. It focuses mainly on permanent buildings, while also considering some rental businesses supplying temporary structures. McKinsey presents modular construction as a possible response to weak construction productivity, labour and housing shortages, programme duration and carbon emissions, but states that cost savings depend on appropriate execution and scale.

The database depicts a fragmented industry. More than 500 identified businesses have less than €50 million in revenue, while a small minority exceed €500 million. Some businesses are long-established or growing, but profitability varies substantially and the industry has also experienced notable failures and bankruptcies. The article segments businesses by value-chain presence, building type, structural material, product type, project complexity, commercial model and geographical presence.

McKinsey reports that companies combining modular manufacture and on-site assembly have higher average margins in its dataset than manufacturing-only companies. It associates this with broader scope and greater control over process, quality, speed and costs. More generally, it argues that close integration or collaboration can align incentives and coordinate design, manufacture, logistics and assembly. This is a correlation and interpretation within the dataset, not proof that vertical integration alone causes better performance.

The article identifies three recurring characteristics among successful modular businesses: a building system at the core, control or close coordination across important parts of the value chain, and focused scaling. Its “building system” is broader than an individual module or product. It comprises the components, design rules, permitted degrees of freedom, manufacturing or procurement arrangements, transport, organisation, assembly procedures and participating companies that together produce a building. Businesses should first establish a robust, repeatable system with sufficient customisation, then continue improving it using lessons from completed projects.

Focused scaling means establishing sufficient and stable demand before adding capacity, and proving the building system and processes on a limited number of projects before expanding across building types or geographies. The article warns that capital-intensive automated factories without adequate demand can fail, and that one poor project can threaten a modular business. Different regulations, building types and local preferences further complicate geographic expansion.

The closing section considers developers, modular manufacturers, contractors and investors, while also identifying roles for design and engineering firms, material producers and distributors. Developers are encouraged to involve modular expertise early; manufacturers to develop systematised processes, partnerships and sensible growth plans; contractors to decide how modular fits their capabilities; and investors to understand the sector’s variation and history of failure before selecting opportunities.

## Main arguments and findings

- The underlying research uses a database of more than 700 companies across more than 50 countries and interviews with more than 20 executives.
- Successful modular businesses commonly place a robust and continuously improving building system at their core.
- A building system includes components, design rules, manufacture or procurement, logistics, assembly, interfaces and the organisations participating in delivery.
- Greater control of, or close coordination across, the value chain is associated with stronger performance in McKinsey’s analysis.
- Product format alone is not decisive: the reported average margins of 2D and 3D producers are relatively close.
- Standardisation, repeatability and focused choices about materials, complexity, building types and geography can support performance.
- Capacity should follow stable demand, and processes should be proven in a focused setting before wider expansion.
- Factory production and automation do not overcome weak demand, fragmented responsibilities or an unproven delivery system.

## Qualifications and limitations

- This is a management analysis based on McKinsey’s proprietary database and industry interviews, not a peer-reviewed study; the underlying company-level data are not published for independent replication.
- Coverage is substantial but incomplete, and several exhibits use smaller subsets for which financial information was available.
- Reported relationships between business characteristics and EBITDA are associations and may be affected by company selection, markets, project mix, accounting and other confounding factors.
- The analysis concentrates on company performance. It does not evaluate housing quality, resident outcomes, architectural quality or the whole-life environmental performance of particular systems.
- Market structure, profitability and technologies can change after the April 2025 database snapshot used in the exhibits.
- McKinsey explicitly says there is no single recipe for success; the three characteristics are recurring tendencies rather than guarantees.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations. McKinsey has not evaluated, endorsed or commented on ExoSett.

- ExoSett can be understood less as a modular building product than as a building system: a common structural and interface framework within which multiple manufacturers, materials, module types and service providers can participate.
- This system-level framing fits ExoSett’s intended distinction between a particular accommodation module and the shared accommodation frames, service frames, structural rules and interfaces that can support many modules and buildings.
- McKinsey’s emphasis on value-chain coordination is relevant to ExoSett’s proposed ecosystem of independent developers, designers, engineers, component and material producers, module manufacturers, contractors, logistics providers and operators.
- Open participation does not eliminate the need for control. ExoSett would require clear interface ownership, assurance, configuration management, responsibilities and feedback from completed projects if independently produced parts are to work together reliably.
- The history of modular-company failures cautions against treating a factory, module or technical platform as a business model. Demand, procurement routes, partnerships, project performance and disciplined scaling remain necessary.
- Focused scaling suggests proving the system with bounded applications and participants before expanding across building types, suppliers or geographies.
