## Synopsis

Shelter argues that ending England's housing emergency requires rebuilding the ability of councils to deliver genuinely affordable social rent homes. The report places council housing within a long-term public investment strategy, rather than treating it principally as a financial liability. It identifies four connected areas for reform: council housing finance, a national council housing mission, land policy and acquisition of existing homes. These measures support an ambition to deliver 90,000 social rent homes annually for ten years across providers and through both new building and acquisition. This is not a target for council new construction alone.

Chapter One (pp. 6-21) proposes cancelling all £31.8 billion of Housing Revenue Account (HRA) debt. HRAs are ring-fenced council landlord accounts funded by tenants' rents. Shelter argues that policy changes, increased costs and additional obligations have undermined the assumptions of the 2012 self-financing settlement. Servicing debt now constrains both maintenance of existing homes and new delivery. Debt cancellation would release borrowing capacity and allow investment in existing stock, but the report explicitly distinguishes this capacity from the additional investment needed to deliver homes.

The report cites commissioned modelling of capacity for over 280,000 additional council social rent homes and a delivery scenario rising to around 45,000 annually. It also advocates bringing forward and increasing Social and Affordable Homes Programme grant funding. Wider fiscal reforms would recognise public housing investment more favourably, including excluding HRAs and public corporations from the Treasury's debt measure. This would enable proposed 0.1% interest loans for council delivery, alongside cheaper and more flexible public borrowing. The report discusses Public Sector Net Worth as a broader way to recognise physical public assets. These are policy proposals and modelled scenarios, not enacted reforms or observed delivery outcomes.

Chapter Two (pp. 22-31) proposes a national mission with an explicit social rent target, delivery agreements with councils in return for debt cancellation, and a council housing champion within central government. National policy should support local delivery and align planning with homelessness, waiting lists and local housing needs. Recommendations include a minimum 20% social rent requirement on medium and large developments, with higher requirements on public land and land released from the green belt. The report calls for rebuilding planning, development and construction expertise, supporting councils through local government reorganisation, pooling resources and establishing a Social Housing Contractor Framework through Homes England. Modern Methods of Construction appear as one possible response to construction skills shortages, rather than the report's principal solution or a prescribed building system.

Chapter Three (pp. 32-39) argues that public land should be managed for long-term public benefit rather than maximum immediate sale receipts. Proposed measures include an optimal-use duty, published land-use management plans and stronger cooperation between public landowners. Social rent delivery should be a priority, with low or no land cost reflected properly in appraisals. Shelter also proposes support for council land assembly, compulsory purchase without speculative future planning uplift or 'hope value', action on stalled sites and wider reform of land and property taxation. Public land is unevenly distributed, so councils also need powers and capacity to assemble other suitable sites.

Chapter Four (pp. 40-47) proposes a separately funded, locally led acquisitions programme lasting at least five years. It would bring long-term empty and neglected private rented properties into permanent social rent use, with funding for renovation as well as purchase. The programme should complement new construction and avoid inflating prices where owner-occupier demand is strong. Recommendations include a national Empty Homes Flying Squad, simpler compulsory purchase procedures for empty homes, support for enforced sale and removal of VAT on renovation for social rent. Shelter allows initial use of acquired homes as temporary accommodation, but proposes conversion to social rent within ten years of purchase. It recognises the immediate practical value of council-owned temporary accommodation while identifying permanent social rent homes as the intended destination.

## Main arguments and findings

- Public-led social rent delivery can address unmet need while supporting construction activity when private development slows.
- HRA debt cancellation, investment and fiscal reform are complementary. Cancellation alone does not fund the construction programme.
- The report presents housing as an enduring public asset whose value includes rental income, avoided public expenditure and wider social and economic benefits.
- National direction needs substantial local authority powers, expertise, resources and democratic responsibility.
- Planning objectives and land valuation should reflect the homes communities need, including homelessness and social housing waiting lists.
- Existing housing can contribute through acquisition, renovation and tenure change, alongside additional new homes. Acquisition increases social housing provision without necessarily increasing the total dwelling stock.
- Pooling council resources, improving procurement certainty and considering off-site construction are parts of rebuilding delivery capacity (pp. 30-31).
- Secure social tenancies and affordable rents matter to long-term stability; the report discusses security, succession and support as well as the physical supply of homes (pp. 42-43).

## Qualifications and limitations

- This is an advocacy and policy report focused on England, drawing on commissioned financial modelling, published evidence and local authority interviews and case studies. It is not an experimental evaluation of the proposed package.
- Fiscal savings, economic returns and delivery capacities are projections dependent on assumptions, investment and implementation. They should be attributed to the report or its commissioned modelling rather than presented as guaranteed outcomes.
- The £31.8 billion debt figure describes the position reported in September 2026. It should not be confused with the original £29.2 billion settlement described by the authors (p. 8).
- The national 90,000 annual ambition and the modelled additional council delivery programme have different scopes. They should not be substituted for one another or added together without explanation.
- The report's descriptions of government policy, fiscal rules and legislation reflect its publication context. Any claim about their current status requires separate verification.
- MMC receives a limited discussion, including the Bristol example and Box 9. The report does not demonstrate the performance of ExoSett, relocatable modules or an adaptable national accommodation stock.
- The separately published annexes and underlying studies would need review before reproducing or assessing detailed modelling claims.
- The report does not endorse ExoSett or the National Accommodation Service (NAS), and it should not be represented as assuming that every public home must remain permanently fixed in one location.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations, not conclusions or endorsements by Shelter.

- ExoSett is an architecture for reconfigurable buildings, with accommodation modules independently supported within engineered exoskeletal frames. Its design separates the support structure from accommodation so modules can be installed, removed and replaced, with relocation a proposed application. Practical relocation programmes and their economic, environmental and delivery performance require demonstration.
- Shelter's emphasis on housing as a lasting public asset creates a useful context for studying long-term adaptability, maintenance, replacement and reuse. These architectural questions complement the report's finance, land and institutional reforms; they cannot replace them.
- The NAS is a related but independent proposal for central government to coordinate a permanent national stock of publicly provided accommodation, with local authorities identifying sites, managing local requirements and overseeing delivery. ExoSett could provide an architecture for elements of that proposal, but neither concept depends on adoption of the other.
- The NAS considers how a public accommodation resource could evolve over decades and generations, including responding to changing geographical demand. Permanence of that resource does not require every building or module to remain at the same location indefinitely.
- Three distinct questions must remain visible: permanence of the public accommodation resource; permanence and security of people's homes; and permanence of the physical location of structures or modules. Physical adaptability must not justify temporary tenure, routine displacement or weaker housing protections.
- Security includes continuity of neighbourhood, employment, education, care and support networks, as well as tenancy arrangements. Decisions about replacement or relocation would require resident involvement and safeguards. Engineering alone cannot secure those outcomes.
- There is substantial common ground in lasting public provision, national coordination and strong local involvement. The NAS raises a related question about adaptability and redistribution of a national resource. It should be presented as a complementary proposal, without implying a deficiency in Shelter's analysis or a shared institutional design.
- Shelter's discussion of pooled capacity, procurement certainty and MMC suggests research into sustained manufacturing demand and compatible components. It does not establish that standardisation or any particular construction architecture will deliver savings or scale successfully.

Constructive questions for further research include:

- How should long-term public value be assessed across maintenance, replacement, reuse and relocation, including transport, site works, disruption and residual value?
- What standards, manufacturing arrangements and governance would enable accommodation components to be reused across sites and suppliers over generations?
- When would redistribution respond usefully to geographical demand, and when would adaptation in place, acquisition or conventional new construction be preferable?
- How can adaptable infrastructure support secure, lasting homes while protecting residents' choices and continuity of community?
