# Increasing Affordable Housing Stock Through Modular Building

## Source

- Slug: `2024-cap-affordable-modular-housing`
- Author / organisation: Michela Zonta, Center for American Progress
- Publication date: 6 February 2024
- Original URL: https://www.americanprogress.org/article/increasing-affordable-housing-stock-through-modular-building/
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

The report examines whether modular building can help address the shortage of affordable housing in the United States. It situates the question within prolonged housing underproduction, rising rents and construction costs, insufficient low-cost rental supply, and changing household composition. Its central argument is conditional: modular building could reduce costs, shorten delivery and improve the environmental performance of new housing, but only if governments and industry overcome barriers that have kept the US sector small.

The report distinguishes several forms of off-site production, including kits of parts, panelised systems, volumetric modules, service pods and manufactured homes. Its main focus is permanent modular construction in which factory-made elements or volumes are transported to a site and assembled as real property under the codes that apply at their final location.

Factory production can allow site work and manufacture to overlap, reduce weather delays, concentrate labour in controlled conditions, improve quality control and make material procurement and use more efficient. The report also associates off-site work with safer working conditions, reduced site disruption, less waste, tighter envelopes and possible energy savings. It argues that shorter programmes and earlier occupancy can matter even where initial costs are comparable with conventional construction.

The report describes modular housing as useful for temporary accommodation serving people experiencing homelessness, students, older adults, migrants and asylum seekers, noting that some modular buildings can be disassembled and redeployed. It also presents flexibility as a long-term design quality. Homes may use universal design, movable partitions or other planned changes to respond to residents’ physical needs, household sizes, family structures and new technologies. Demographic ageing, single-person and extended households, and changes in how homes are used strengthen the case for adaptable housing.

Adoption remains limited. Modular production needs substantial early payments for materials and factory work, while conventional construction finance releases funds against site-based milestones and treats completed work as collateral. Manufacturers are scarce and geographically concentrated. Volumetric modules are bulky, may be damaged during handling and must comply with differing state transport limits and permits. Building-code administration, inspections, approvals and zoning vary across states and localities. The report also discusses workforce and wage concerns, weak production pipelines, factory capacity and stigma associated with prefabricated housing.

Its policy recommendations call for more suitable public and private finance, support for energy-efficient affordable modular projects, more consistent state approval and code rules, harmonised transport requirements, streamlined local planning, workforce development and prevailing wages where manufacturers receive public support, and grants, tax credits or public purchasing that help expand manufacturing capacity.

## Main arguments and findings

- Modular construction is a promising but partial supply-side response to US housing affordability.
- Factory manufacture may reduce site labour, overlap work stages, shorten construction programmes, improve quality control and reduce material waste.
- Speed and earlier occupancy may create value even when headline construction costs are not dramatically lower.
- Modular housing can answer temporary accommodation needs and can also incorporate longer-term flexibility for changing households, physical needs and technologies.
- Financing and payment structures, fragmented codes and approvals, zoning, transport constraints, limited manufacturing capacity, workforce questions and public perceptions inhibit wider adoption.
- Achieving scale requires coordinated government and industry action rather than construction technology alone.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The report is a policy analysis and advocacy document, not a systematic review or controlled comparison of completed projects.
- Many performance and cost estimates are drawn from industry, consultancy, government and secondary sources and will vary by project type, location and market conditions.
- Its statistical analysis concerns the US housing market and cannot be transferred directly to other countries.
- The report addresses several forms of off-site construction; their economics, transport needs and capacity for later change are not identical.
- Flexibility is discussed broadly and does not establish that every conventionally modular building is readily reconfigured or relocated.
- Increasing construction supply does not by itself ensure that completed homes are affordable to the households most in need.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations. The report does not discuss or endorse ExoSett.

- The report supports a useful distinction between modular construction as an initial production method and modularity retained as a building changes through life.
- Conventional modular buildings or major volumes may sometimes be redeployed. ExoSett instead proposes retaining accommodation and service frames while smaller accommodation modules are installed, removed, replaced or refurbished.
- Discussion of homelessness, ageing, household composition and adaptable homes provides independent context for potential social-housing, temporary-accommodation and retirement-living applications.
- The identified barriers remain relevant: ExoSett would still require appropriate finance, approvals, transport planning, manufacturing capacity and public trust.
- Further work would need to demonstrate lifecycle cost, disruption during module changes, logistics, resident experience and whether adaptation produces genuine affordability or social benefit.
