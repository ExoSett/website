Modular construction and affordable housing supply

A US housing-policy report considers how factory production could help increase the supply of affordable homes, and what prevents modular construction from reaching scale.

Increasing Affordable Housing Stock Through Modular Building was written by Michela Zonta and published by the Center for American Progress in February 2024. The discussion of ExoSett below is independent commentary inspired by the report; neither the author nor the Center for American Progress has considered or endorsed ExoSett.

Original source

Center for American Progress · February 2024

Increasing Affordable Housing Stock Through Modular Building

Michela Zonta · Center for American Progress report · 6 February 2024

Read the original report at the Center for American Progress

The report’s argument

A promising tool whose benefits depend upon reaching scale

The report argues that modular construction could help increase affordable housing supply in the United States. Moving work into factories can reduce site labour, allow site preparation and building production to proceed together, shorten construction programmes and improve cost predictability. Controlled production can also support quality control, safer working conditions, more precise use of materials and less construction waste.

It does not present modular building as a complete answer to housing affordability. Wider adoption is constrained by financing models that do not suit substantial early factory payments, fragmented building codes and approvals, differing transport rules and physical transport limits, restricted manufacturing capacity and negative public perceptions. The report recommends coordinated changes to finance, regulation, workforce policy and industrial capacity.

Housing that can respond

Temporary needs and longer-term change

The report describes modular homes being used for short-term accommodation needs, including for people experiencing homelessness, students, older people, migrants and asylum seekers. Some conventional modular buildings can be disassembled and redeployed elsewhere.

Its idea of flexibility is wider than relocation. Housing can be designed to respond to changing physical needs, household sizes, family structures and technologies. Ageing populations, more single-person and extended households, and changing activities within the home make that capacity increasingly relevant.

These observations provide independent background relevant to the retirement-living and temporary or social-housing possibilities considered elsewhere on this site. That relevance is ExoSett’s interpretation, not a claim made about ExoSett by the report.

What ExoSett finds interesting

A production method or a continuing property

Modular construction is often understood principally as a way of producing a building. ExoSett is particularly interested in modularity as a continuing property of a building throughout its life.

Conventional modular approaches may allow a complete modular building, or substantial parts of one, to be relocated or reconfigured. ExoSett instead proposes a long-lived accommodation frame and service frame. Individual accommodation modules could be installed, removed, replaced or taken away for refurbishment without replacing or relocating the building itself.

Conventional modular construction can make housing quicker and more economical to produce. ExoSett extends the idea by separating long-lived building infrastructure from the accommodation it supports, allowing individual accommodation modules to change as needs change.

Limits and dependencies

A building system does not remove delivery barriers

The report’s account of finance, approvals, transport, manufacturing capacity and perception is also a useful warning. ExoSett’s different arrangement does not make those questions disappear. A viable implementation would still need project finance, regulatory approval, suitable logistics, capable manufacturers and public confidence.

Nor does adaptability itself create affordable housing. Land, planning, funding, tenure, operating costs and housing policy remain decisive. ExoSett proposes a way for building infrastructure and accommodation to change on different timescales; its affordability and social value would have to be established in particular projects.
