Research and Reading

From reusable to reused

Modularity alone does not produce circularity. ExoSett’s stronger objective is components that can be recovered, assessed, accepted and usefully redeployed, with their identity and engineering history intact.

This independent discussion draws on Tsavdaridis and colleagues’ review of timber and steel modular buildings. The ExoSett interpretations below do not imply endorsement by the authors or publisher.

Original source

Developments in the Built Environment · 16 November 2026

Circularity in Timber and Steel Modular Buildings: Adaptability, Deconstruction and Reuse

Konstantinos Daniel Tsavdaridis, Dan-Adrian Corfar, Zhengyao Li, Stylianos Kallioras and Dionysios Bournas · article 101113 (2026) · DOI: 10.1016/j.dibe.2026.101113

Read the open-access journal paper

Accepted manuscript at City Research Online

The research

Modularity is not reuse

The review distinguishes intended, simulated, physically tested and commercially reported reuse. Relocatable steel buildings already have established reuse pathways, although the review’s evidence comes from suppliers without connection-level performance data. Elsewhere, repeated reuse remains much less well demonstrated.

The distinction is practical. The authors report that PLACE/Ladywell’s intended relocation was frustrated by condition, cost and regulatory changes. A timber demonstrator achieved one building-scale dismantling and reassembly after earthquake testing; M-Box1.0’s 100 reassembly cycles were virtual. Neither establishes repeated service lives for a complete building, including its enclosure and services.

This is a non-systematic review supported by an indicative survey of 33 respondents, predominantly in Europe, with no contractors or clients. It identifies mechanisms and evidence gaps, rather than measuring industry-wide success rates. Its regulatory recommendations focus on EU frameworks (sections 2, 4–8; Tables 4–5).

Relevance to ExoSett

Design the route to the next use

A removable bolt is insufficient if finishes, fire-stopping or services prevent access. The paper treats demountability as a property of the whole system and discusses host frames with interchangeable modules. ExoSett’s proposed architecture responds to these issues: the accommodation frame provides primary support, pods are independently removable rather than structurally stacked, and the service frame separates utilities and access from accommodation. Standardised interfaces are intended to make exchange practical.

These choices address barriers; they do not prove successful reuse. Removal sequences, temporary support, service isolation and protection of the remaining building still need engineering and testing. Continued use of the frame does not promise uninterrupted occupation nearby.

The intended unit of recovery also matters. A pod, beam, column or frame-node assembly may be reused intact while seals, fasteners or wear items are replaced. Our interpretation is that reversibility belongs at the intended recovery interfaces: every internal joint need not be separable for an assembly to remain useful.

Reuse preserves functioning components and their embodied engineering value; recycling recovers constituent materials. ExoSett aims to retain that component value where safe and worthwhile, while recognising that repair, transport, storage and the next application determine the economic and environmental outcome.

ExoSett information model

Information follows the component

The paper calls for traceable geometry, tolerances, connections, loading history, inspections and custody records. ExoSett’s information-model approach separates three responsibilities:

  • Building Class Document: an immutable version of a building class, defining or importing component classes, positions, interfaces, rules and design approvals.
  • Building Instance Document: a record of one physical building at a particular time, including the components occupying its positions, site configuration and approvals.
  • Component Instance Document: the continuing identity and history of one physical item, covering manufacture, deployment, inspection, removal, storage, repair, condition, recertification and reuse.

A removed beam should not become anonymous material. Its record can support reassessment, but documentation alone does not establish fitness for another use, and a class approval does not automatically approve a new deployment. ExoSett intends to prefer suitable international standards for identification, reference designation and information exchange, so records can remain interoperable beyond ExoSett itself. This document architecture is ExoSett’s proposal, not the paper’s.

From reusable to reused

The eventual test is a working lifecycle: design → manufacture → installation → operation → removal → inspection → repair or recertification where required → redeployment.

Evidence must cover repeated connection and disconnection without unacceptable degradation; reliable condition assessment and persistent identity; practical removal, transport, storage and installation; and continuing engineering acceptance with predictable structural and service performance. There must also be a viable next use, with responsibility and costs allocated.

Event accommodation illustrates planned dismantling and redeployment of an entire frame and its pods. Permanent accommodation may benefit more from selective pod replacement or service upgrades while the frame remains useful. Extending service life is valuable too: movement is not the sole measure of circularity.

What ExoSett takes from this

Modularity should not be treated as evidence of circularity. For ExoSett, the stronger objective is repeatable reuse. The physical system, interfaces, records, inspection, approvals and operating procedures must work together. This is a design objective requiring demonstration, not a claim that ExoSett has already achieved repeated reuse.
