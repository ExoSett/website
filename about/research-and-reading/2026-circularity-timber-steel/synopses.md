## Synopsis

The paper examines circularity in timber and steel volumetric modular systems at building-system, module/sub-assembly and connection levels. It asks what reuse has actually been demonstrated, how connections influence recovery, what prevents repeated reuse and what standards and documentation could help. Its central distinction is between modularity as an enabling architecture and circular outcomes established through evidence.

The review draws on 180 sources from 1995–2026, approximately 80% from 2020 onwards. Sources include research, standards, policy, project descriptions and supplier material. The authors explicitly state that there was no systematic database search or formal screening. Panelised, post-and-beam and conventional structural examples are included where they explain mechanisms relevant to volumetric reuse.

An online purposive survey, distributed through professional networks and project partners between September and November 2025, provides indicative perceptions. There were 33 respondents: 10 researchers/academics, nine structural engineers, six manufacturers/suppliers, four designers/architects and four other roles. No contractors or clients responded. Twenty-eight worked primarily in Europe. The response rate is unknown; individual items have different denominators and multi-select totals can exceed 100%.

## Main arguments and findings

### Circularity beyond construction waste (sections 1–3)

Reduced site waste does not establish lower whole-life impacts. Benefits depend on material efficiency, manufacturing, transport, service-life assumptions, recovery and the next use. Reuse and extended useful life retain more component function than material recycling, although environmental outcomes remain scenario-dependent. The paper considers adaptation and prolonged service alongside relocation.

### Different scales and strengths of evidence (section 4; Tables 2–5)

- Relocatable steel suppliers provide established commercial reuse examples, but the review's redeployment evidence is supplier-sourced and lacks connection-level performance data.
- PLACE/Ladywell was intended for relocation but was not relocated; the manuscript describes cost, regulatory change and fabric deterioration as barriers. Y:Cube's continued use demonstrates service life, not demonstrated relocation.
- A full-scale JRC CLT rocking prototype was earthquake-tested, dismantled and reassembled in a modified configuration. The synthesis identifies one building-scale disassembly/reassembly. This is stronger than a design claim but does not establish repeated long-term service cycles.
- Recovered CLT panels used in a Japanese café needed grading and adaptation of the new design to available stock. About 27% were discarded, and inadequate storage caused swelling. Recovery, storage and fit for the next use materially affect yield.
- M-Box1.0 includes 100 virtual reassembly cycles; reassembly testing did not cover enclosure waterproofing and insulation. The Legacy Living Lab assigned some envelope materials to recycling or landfill. Structural reuse is not proof of complete-building reuse.
- Host structures with interchangeable modules are explicitly discussed in section 4.2.1, citing Di Pasquale et al. (2020) and Rana and Singh (2023). This is relevant prior work for ExoSett, not evidence of ExoSett's novelty.

### Connection mechanisms (section 5)

Reversible connections require access, controlled release, tolerance management and acceptable performance after reassembly. Timber recovery can be limited by moisture, crushing, splitting, damaged holes and concealed fasteners. Steel systems face seized fasteners, distortion, corrosion, jamming and integration with fire-stopping, services and fit-out. Dry bolting helps but does not guarantee practical dismantling.

Replaceable connector parts can protect more valuable primary members. The review discusses timber connection hierarchies that concentrate damage in replaceable steel parts and conventional steel seats, collars, clamps and slotted interfaces. Welding is not described as an absolute prohibition on reuse: cutting can permit recovery with cost, risk and section loss. The level at which an assembly will be recovered matters.

The HyMC example reused three joint assemblies and bearings across six cyclic tests, replacing bolting assemblies. This is joint-level evidence; cyclic structural loading and assembly/disassembly cycles must not be conflated. The paper calls for qualification protocols measuring residual stiffness, slip and hole damage through repeated assembly cycles.

### Information, assessment and markets (sections 6–8)

Survey respondents identify standardisation, structural integrity verification, disassembly/transport/reconditioning cost and approval barriers. The 33-person sample cannot establish industry-wide rankings or commercial feasibility. Figure 15 shows mixed preferences for reuse scale; 45.5% select a hybrid approach for future circularity.

The recommendations link three strands: specifications for assessing used/remanufactured products, environmental declarations with explicit reuse scenarios, and digital product-passport content supporting inspection and recertification. Suggested records include as-built geometry and tolerances, connection inventories, fastener specifications, loading history, inspection evidence and chain of custody. These are proposed enablers, not a validated complete information system or automatic approval process.

## Qualifications and limitations

The non-systematic source selection cannot establish exhaustive coverage or universal absence of reuse. Some prominent technical examples arise from the authors' own research; the methods disclose that their research informed selection. Evidence is heterogeneous: intentions, simulations, component tests, building demonstrations and supplier reports must retain their labels.

The small, predominantly European survey is non-representative and omits contractors and clients, limiting conclusions about practical commercial arrangements. Recommendations primarily concern EU instruments; they should not be restated as legal advice or automatically transferred to UK approvals. No independent legal audit or replication of cited experiments was undertaken for this entry.

Some affirmative wording in individual case descriptions is more cautious in the synthesis: one successful reassembly is not repeated service-life validation. Modelled optimal cycle counts or carbon savings are scenario-specific, not general limits or forecasts for ExoSett. Long-lived in-situ use can be a good outcome without relocation.

## Relevance to ExoSett

**The following is ExoSett's interpretation, not the authors' evaluation or endorsement.**

The separation of primary accommodation frame, independently removable pods and service/access infrastructure responds to the paper's system-integration concerns. Standardised interfaces may support exchange, but removal access, temporary works, service isolation, fire/acoustic performance, tolerances and handling still require engineering and evidence.

Define recovery units explicitly. A pod or frame-node assembly may be reused intact while seals, bolts or wear items are replaced; making every internal joint reversible is not necessarily required. Beams, columns and frame assemblies may also be recovered individually. Event accommodation provides a plausible whole-building redeployment use case; permanent buildings may gain more from selective replacement and continued frame service.

The paper supports persistent physical-component records. ExoSett's Building Class Document, Building Instance Document and Component Instance Document are its own architecture: immutable class versions establish definitions and rules; building-instance revisions record physical occupancy and site configuration; component-instance revisions preserve identity and life history. Complete records and class membership are not proof of current fitness or approval of a future deployment. Suitable international identification, reference-designation and exchange standards should support interoperability beyond ExoSett.

A credible next use requires a recipient, an acceptable condition and a workable allocation of cost and responsibility, as well as technical recoverability. There is no claim that ExoSett has experimentally demonstrated repeated reuse, superior performance or a novel combination untried by predecessors.
