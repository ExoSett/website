Why offsite construction isn’t working: lessons for ExoSett

James Broadhead’s critique of offsite construction raises a useful question for ExoSett: could a common architecture reduce delivery risks while preserving a building’s capacity to change?

This is an independent ExoSett interpretation of practitioner opinion, not scientific proof. Broadhead does not discuss or endorse ExoSett.

Original source

REMI Network · 3 November 2026

Why offsite construction isn’t working

James Broadhead · Construction Business, REMI Network. The author is a partner of IGV Housing and COO of IGV Build Systems.

Read the original article at REMI Network

The practitioner perspective

Factory production does not settle delivery

Broadhead identifies factory queues, early design freezes and costly revisions following approvals. Faster enclosure can leave services and interiors proceeding slowly; tight tolerances and coordination remain demanding. He also describes a funding mismatch when lenders recognise installed work but not production in a factory.

His response is hybrid construction: select methods where they help, coordinate disciplines and involve approval bodies early. These observations challenge blanket promises of speed and savings; they do not establish that every offsite project fails.

ExoSett’s response

Standardise interfaces; retain the capacity to change

ExoSett is an architecture for reconfigurable buildings. Standardised frames, nodes and interfaces are intended to accommodate independently manufactured, interchangeable pods. Each accommodation frame is paired with a service frame providing continuing access and utility routes.

Settling recurring requirements at system level could reduce repeated design work and allow structure, services and accommodation to be procured as coordinated packages. It would move some decisions earlier, not remove the need for design freezes or site-specific coordination. Factories would still need capacity and dependable demand.

Multiple competing suppliers working to common specifications could reduce dependence on one manufacturer. That requires maintained standards, clear responsibilities, warranties and reliable conformity checks. The existing discussion of McKinsey’s building-system analysis also highlights the importance of coordination and proving a system before scaling it.

Demonstrating compatibility

A recorded specification and physical checks

The ExoSett XML design document records the building arrangement. The fuller specification could define or reference dimensions, tolerances, interfaces and verification requirements, giving future suppliers a maintained basis for assessing compatibility. Valid XML alone does not establish engineering conformity.

Physical jigs, themselves checked against the applicable specification, could verify critical dimensions, connection positions and clearances before installation. They would complement structural, fire, acoustic and other performance assessments. The fuller specifications and jig procedures remain under development; interchangeability across independent manufacturers has not yet been demonstrated.

Potential benefits of maturity

Reducing repeated uncertainty

Planning. Established structural solutions could simplify technical preparation and leave greater attention for site-specific issues such as layout, appearance, access and local impact. That is a possible efficiency in developing proposals, not a promise of planning consent.

Regulatory compliance. Repeatable components and interfaces could allow relevant test evidence and certification to support later assessments, reducing duplicated effort. Each application and configuration would still need assessment against its applicable requirements.

Financing. Demonstrated methods, predictable costs and established supply chains could make projects easier for lenders and insurers to assess. Familiarity would not itself resolve factory-stage funding, ownership of unfinished components or supplier failure risk.

Beyond initial construction

Whole-life value must be demonstrated

ExoSett’s reconfigurability is intended to let accommodation be replaced, upgraded or rearranged while retaining useful structure and shared infrastructure. That could extend value beyond the initial construction programme as needs change.

The economic comparison must include the upfront cost of enabling change, subsequent maintenance, replacement logistics, disruption and actual reuse. A capacity to adapt has value only where it can be exercised practically and economically.

ExoSett seeks to address some structural weaknesses of conventional modular construction through standardisation, interoperability and whole-life reconfigurability. Engineering validation, commercial demonstration, an effective supply ecosystem and measured economic evidence must establish whether those advantages materialise.
