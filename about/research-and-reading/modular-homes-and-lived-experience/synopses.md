# Modular Homes as a New Form of Accommodation to Tackle Homelessness: A Case Study From Cambridge, England

## Source

- Slug: `modular-homes-and-lived-experience`
- Authors / organisation: Richmond Juvenile Ehwi, Kwadwo Oti-Sarpong, Gemma Burgess, Johannes Lenhard and Eana Meng; published in *Human Ecology*
- Publication date: 12 April 2023 (online publication)
- Original URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10090745/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10745-023-00404-1
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

This exploratory qualitative case study examines the first six self-contained modular homes provided for people experiencing homelessness in Cambridge, England. The authors are interested in what residents’ lived experience can teach organisations considering similar projects, not in measuring the scheme against a controlled comparator or claiming that modular homes solve homelessness. They combine longitudinal interviews with all six initial residents and contextual information about the project and its support network.

The approximately 25-square-metre furnished homes each provided a private bathroom, kitchen and living/bedroom space, an individual front door and letterbox. The site also had a shared garden and cycle storage. Residents were selected through the local homelessness-support ecosystem and chose to participate after pre-tenancy discussions. Their licences included restrictions on illegal drugs and alcohol and the possibility of checks. Wrap-around help was central to the model, including support with tenancy, money, health, substance use, employment and relationships. The authors therefore consistently attribute outcomes to the combination of physical accommodation, residents’ efforts and support, rather than to the modules alone.

Residents described privacy, safety and control associated with having their own front door, particularly compared with rough sleeping or shared provision. Over the study period, interview evidence indicated improvements in managing drug or alcohol use and money, paying rent, developing skills and considering work. Some residents renewed family connections or built better relations with neighbours and other residents. The small group developed elements of community while retaining individual space. A central, serviced location made amenities and opportunities accessible and enabled contact with the surrounding community.

The paper also finds varied trajectories. Residents did not all progress at the same pace or want the same next step. Some wished to remain in the modular homes beyond the planned three-year period or to move with a unit if it were relocated. One resident had previously found a larger conventional flat difficult to manage. The authors use this to question a one-size-fits-all understanding of independent housing and suggest that self-contained modular homes could, for some people, form part of longer-term or move-on options.

Important tensions remain. The project resembles a treatment-first model because continuing accommodation was linked to behavioural conditions and support, although entry was voluntary. Dependence on temporary or “meanwhile” sites creates uncertainty, and equally well-located land may not be available elsewhere. If residents must leave at the end of a planning or tenancy period before they are ready, gains may be reversed. The approach also does not address structural causes of homelessness or replace the need for affordable social housing.

The authors conclude that the Cambridge experience shows promising short- and medium-term outcomes from a private, self-contained home combined with sustained support. They call for research across larger and more diverse populations, different site sizes, accommodation designs and support levels. The paper presents the scheme as one option within a wider response to homelessness, with implementation details and individual needs more important than the modular label alone.

## Main arguments and findings

- Residents reported greater privacy, security and autonomy from self-contained homes with individual front doors.
- Accommodation plus wrap-around support was associated with improved substance-use and money management, work readiness, relationships and community.
- A central, connected site supported access to services and interaction with the surrounding neighbourhood.
- Residents had different ambitions and levels of readiness; conventional independent housing was not automatically the best next step for everyone.
- Modular homes may be useful as temporary or move-on accommodation, but their role depends on support, tenure, location and individual circumstances.
- Similar projects require more extensive comparative and longitudinal research.

## Qualifications and limitations

- Six residents at one Cambridge site form a very small, non-diverse case study.
- The research is exploratory and expressly not an effectiveness evaluation.
- Interview accounts show perceived change and experience, not causal attribution to construction method.
- Housing, support, licence conditions, personal motivation and location operated together and cannot readily be separated.
- COVID-19 restrictions required video interviews during part of the work.
- The paper records short- and medium-term outcomes; long-term housing stability was unresolved.
- The cohort and selection process limit generalisation to families, women, different ethnic groups or people with different support needs.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations.

- The study supports treating privacy, autonomy, safety, personalisation and an individual front door as core housing qualities, not secondary benefits of a construction system.
- It exposes a risk in crediting physical modularity for outcomes produced by residents, support organisations, tenancy arrangements and a good site.
- Varied housing trajectories support ExoSett’s interest in accommodation that can change, but do not demonstrate that physical reconfiguration improves resident outcomes.
- ExoSett should investigate how adaptation can occur without undermining tenure or forcing residents to move when sites or modules change.
- Resident choice, support provision, domestic control, location and long-term affordability require as much attention as engineering interfaces.
