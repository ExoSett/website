# Could modular construction spark a housing revolution?

## Source

- Slug: `2019-arup-housing-revolution`
- Author / organisation: Vicky Evans, Arup
- Publication date: August 2019 (listed as “Last updated”)
- Original URL: https://www.arup.com/insights/could-modular-construction-spark-a-housing-revolution/
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

Vicky Evans presents modern methods of construction (MMC), used in the article as broadly synonymous with modular construction, as an opportunity to reconsider how UK housing is conceived, designed, approved and built. The article starts from the need for substantially more housing and the observation that, despite long-standing interest in MMC, less than 10% of UK housing was then built using these methods. It notes that financial and technical barriers had received attention, but concentrates on the planning system as another obstacle to wider adoption.

The first proposal is to make development management more predictable. Evans suggests that modular homes could use pre-agreed design specifications and design codes defining a range of acceptable options. Used with Local Development Orders, this might allow some applications to be determined quickly and reduce risk for manufacturers and developers. This depends on behavioural and institutional change: the promised efficiency would be lost if planning officers or elected members routinely sought project-by-project alterations, or if planning conditions were applied without pragmatism.

The article does not equate factory production with architectural uniformity. It argues that successful MMC housing must appeal both to occupants and to existing communities. House types, materials, massing, layouts and details can vary in response to local context. Standard components and controlled manufacture are therefore presented as compatible with variety. The article also envisages adaptable homes that respond to changing needs, including ageing, and compares future domestic upgrading with upgrading cars or technology. Examples of large firms investing in prefabricated housing and manufacturers developing adaptable systems illustrate the direction of travel rather than proving a particular delivery model.

Digital methods form the next part of the argument. Evans describes mass customisation: manufacturers producing a wide range of homes from common components, with residents potentially configuring homes online. Digital designs that comply with pre-agreed rules could move directly from a platform through planning and into production. The intended result is an integrated, less paper-dependent process rather than merely the digital drafting of conventional applications.

Finally, the article says that MMC cannot scale through a manufacturer acting alone. Local authorities, developers, planners, public bodies and manufacturers need sufficient common demand and aligned processes. Planning is treated as one part of a wider housing system whose participants must cooperate to create critical mass, change attitudes and make modular delivery routine.

The piece is a short professional argument, not an empirical study. It sets out possibilities and enabling conditions rather than testing whether the proposed planning, digital or manufacturing arrangements deliver the claimed results.

## Main arguments and findings

- MMC could speed housing delivery, make difficult sites viable and provide varied, adaptable homes.
- Pre-agreed specifications, design codes and Local Development Orders could make planning decisions faster and more predictable.
- Industrialised construction need not produce identical homes; materials, form and layout can respond to place.
- Common components and digital configuration could support mass customisation.
- Adaptable modular homes could respond to residents’ changing needs, including ageing.
- Adoption at scale requires coordinated action by manufacturers, developers, councils, planners and other public bodies.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The article is an expert viewpoint and contains no original methods, comparative evaluation or resident evidence.
- Most benefits are framed as opportunities or potential outcomes, not demonstrated findings.
- Its market share and housing-need figures describe 2019 and may no longer be current.
- The streamlined planning model depends on agreement about acceptable variation and on institutional behaviour changing.
- The analogy with upgrading consumer products does not examine tenure, affordability, disruption, durability or residents’ control over changes.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations, not claims made about ExoSett by Evans or Arup.

- The argument supports ExoSett’s emphasis on standardising interfaces and constraints without standardising complete buildings.
- Mass customisation and machine-readable, pre-agreed rules suggest a possible route from ExoSett specifications to design, approval and manufacture.
- Adaptability aligns with ExoSett, although ExoSett places more emphasis on physical change after first construction than the article develops.
- The need for cross-sector coordination warns that a sound component system alone cannot create a viable housing market or planning pathway.
- Further research is needed on who controls post-occupancy adaptation, how changes are approved and financed, and whether repeatable interfaces remain useful across manufacturers and generations of components.

## Points reflected on the website

The public page discusses industrialisation with local variety; standardisation without sameness; adaptable homes; digital configuration and machine-readable rules; faster, more predictable planning; and the partnerships needed for scale. It explicitly distinguishes Arup’s general MMC argument from ExoSett’s additional ambition to industrialise subsequent building change.
