# Insights into the use of modular housing in addressing homelessness

## Source

- Slug: `modular-housing-and-homelessness`
- Authors / organisation: Dr Katy Karampour and Dr Gemma Burgess; Centre for Homelessness Impact, with the Cambridge Centre for Housing and Planning Research
- Publication date: April 2022
- Original URL: https://www.landecon.cam.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-05/Insights%20into%20the%20use%20of%20modular%20housing%20in%20addressing%20homelessness%20report.pdf
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

This report maps UK schemes using self-contained purpose-built modular units or converted shipping containers as temporary accommodation for people who have experienced, or are at risk of, homelessness. It is an initial evidence-building exercise: the authors aim to describe existing provision, identify potential benefits and barriers, and create a typology that later research can use to compare effectiveness and residents’ outcomes. Sleeping pods and modular general-needs or ordinary affordable housing are outside scope.

The research used desk-based internet searches, published and media material, planning documents and contact with local authorities and other stakeholders. It identified 33 schemes in 22 local-authority areas, comprising 808 units: 27 modular schemes and six converted-container schemes. Of the units, 427 housed families and 381 single households. The schemes were classified by construction type, site size (below 20 units or at least 20) and resident cohort. The authors regard these characteristics as plausible influences on outcomes rather than as proven causal factors. Container schemes were larger on average than purpose-built modular schemes, while small modular schemes for single people were the largest category.

All identified projects were intended as transitional “move-on” accommodation, normally with tenancies of no more than two years and support intended to help residents reach permanent independent housing. In practice, moves could be delayed because long-term affordable housing was unavailable. Eleven schemes had temporary planning permission on “meanwhile” sites, fourteen had permanent permission and information was unavailable for eight. Local authorities owned the land in 70% of cases. Information about resident support was missing for most schemes.

Available unit-size data raised concern. For 11 schemes it was unavailable; among the remaining 22, 18 were below the nationally described space standard and four met it. The report notes that the standard operates through planning rather than building regulations, but treats size as an important subject for resident-outcome research. Location information was also incomplete. Anecdotal material suggested that some readily available sites were distant from shops, employment, schools or other services. Some projects lacked communal or play facilities, particularly consequential for families and children.

The report sets out possible advantages rather than verified effects. Factory production and installation may be faster and cheaper than new conventional social housing; units may use under-utilised land, be relocated, and offer greater privacy or quality of life than hostels or shared accommodation. Robust cost evidence was scarce, however, and resident experience had not yet been studied systematically. Project delivery remained complex, requiring land, planning, building-control, finance, design, site works and coordination among multiple organisations.

Barriers included finding suitable sites, obtaining capital, community opposition and assembling viable partnerships. Limitations included small dwellings, weak locations, design quality and absence of facilities. The report’s central conclusion is therefore not that modular accommodation is effective, but that provision is diverse, evidence is thin and systematic evaluation—especially listening to residents—is necessary before relative effectiveness can be established.

## Main arguments and findings

- The mapping found 33 schemes, 22 local authorities and 808 units.
- Purpose-built modular schemes and converted containers should not be treated as one uniform intervention.
- Construction type, scheme size and household type form an initial typology for later comparison.
- Most schemes with known dimensions fell below national space standards.
- Potential benefits include delivery speed, portability, use of under-used land, privacy and possible improvement over shared temporary accommodation.
- Suitable land, funding, planning, regulation, coordination and local acceptance remain substantial barriers.
- There was insufficient evidence on costs, resident experience and outcomes to judge effectiveness.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The work is mainly a desk-based mapping exercise, not an outcome or cost-effectiveness evaluation.
- Publicly available information was incomplete, particularly for unit sizes, locations and support services.
- Benefits are explicitly described as potential and require further research.
- Schemes were recent when studied, limiting evidence about durability, relocation, long-term costs and move-on outcomes.
- The scope excludes sleeping pods, general-needs modular housing and conventional affordable-housing programmes.
- Counts and scheme status reflect the research period and may since have changed.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following is ExoSett’s interpretation.

- The typology supports separating construction method, site design, scale, users and support model rather than treating “modular housing” as a single product.
- Findings on small units, weak locations and missing amenities expose the risk of optimising speed or available land at the expense of adequate housing.
- Portability is relevant to ExoSett, but the report warns that mobility does not make an unsuitable site or insecure tenure adequate.
- ExoSett’s separable frames, services and accommodation could offer alternatives to moving whole dwellings, but this remains an untested proposition.
- Future ExoSett research should include resident outcomes, whole-life cost, space and amenity, service provision, disruption during change, and the governance of temporary versus permanent use.

## Points reflected on the website

The public page reflects the 33-scheme mapping, distinction between modular units and containers, scale and cohort differences, the prevalence of sub-standard unit sizes, location and amenity concerns, possible speed and cost benefits, portability, complex delivery barriers, short tenancies and the lack of robust outcome evidence. It uses these points to argue that modularity, small size and temporariness are not themselves housing objectives.
