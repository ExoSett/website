Housing-led approaches need homes—and more than homes

Research into housing-led policy in the Netherlands shows why a permanent dwelling is necessary, but cannot by itself provide support, autonomy or housing security.

Housing the homeless: Shifting sites of managing the poor in the Netherlands was written by Nienke Fredrika Boesveldt and Dolly Loomans of the University of Amsterdam and published in Urban Studies, volume 61, issue 7, in 2024. It was first published online on 29 November 2023. This independent ExoSett discussion does not imply endorsement by the authors, the University of Amsterdam, Urban Studies or SAGE.

Housing, care and circumstances

A dwelling is necessary, but it does not act alone

The paper examines how housing-led approaches were implemented amid shortages of affordable housing and reductions in health and social care. Its interviews show the interactions among housing availability, support, tenancy conditions, institutional responsibilities and individual circumstances. A regular home is a major improvement on shelters and institutions, but providing a dwelling does not settle those wider questions.

ExoSett should keep the same boundary clear. It is a proposed building system, not a homelessness policy, care service or tenancy model. It cannot determine who receives housing, provide appropriate support, secure an affordable rent or protect a resident’s rights. Those responsibilities remain with the people and institutions that plan, fund, allocate, operate and support housing.

ExoSett addresses accommodation, not homelessness itself.

Permanent housing requires supply

Housing-led approaches need suitable homes to lead with

The authors describe a severe shortage of affordable housing in the Netherlands. In that context, access to independent housing was restricted, waiting times were long and priority systems could leave people caught between shelters, care institutions and an unaffordable private market.

Housing supply is not the whole response to homelessness, but a shortage of suitable affordable and social housing constrains any approach that depends on permanent homes. ExoSett’s possible relevance is limited to this physical-infrastructure problem: it may offer another way to create, adapt, refurbish and manage permanent accommodation. Whether that accommodation is affordable, appropriately located and made available through fair and secure arrangements would depend on the project around it.

A home is not a placement

Privacy, autonomy and security are part of the accommodation

The paper warns that a permanent address does not necessarily become a secure home. Conditional leases, surveillance, threats of eviction and unclear divisions between care and control can extend institutional management into private space. The authors describe the home as a possible base for freedom, belonging and security, while showing how insecure arrangements can undermine those qualities.

This is an important design lesson for any future ExoSett social-housing or homelessness example. Accommodation should not be conceived merely as a unit into which somebody can be placed. Its layout, entrances, services, management and means of adaptation should respect domestic privacy, resident control and the ability to belong in a home and neighbourhood. Physical design cannot guarantee autonomy or security of tenure, but it can either support or frustrate them.

Permanent homes, adaptable buildings

The building system changes; the resident’s home need not become provisional

ExoSett’s removable accommodation and separable structure and services should not imply that residents occupy temporary or lesser homes. Adaptability is a property of the building system: it may allow accommodation to be created, maintained, refurbished or altered as needs change.

Any adaptation during occupation would still need careful design and management. It should not weaken tenure, make relocation routine or give an operator greater control over a resident’s private life. A technically changeable building can support permanent housing only when change serves the quality and continuity of the home.

Permanent accommodation can be supported by adaptable infrastructure.

What ExoSett takes from this

Keep the physical contribution in proportion

The paper’s account of housing-led policy makes the limit of a building-system proposal especially clear. ExoSett may contribute to the supply and stewardship of suitable permanent accommodation. It would not solve homelessness, implement Housing First, provide health or social care, establish tenancy rights or answer the wider questions of poverty, affordability and public policy.

The narrower contribution still matters because housing-led approaches require homes. It should be assessed by whether the resulting accommodation supports privacy, security, autonomy and belonging—not simply by how efficiently a module can be produced or moved.
