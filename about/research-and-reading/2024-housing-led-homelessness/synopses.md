# Housing the homeless: Shifting sites of managing the poor in the Netherlands

## Source

- Slug: `2024-housing-led-homelessness`
- Authors / organisation: Nienke Fredrika Boesveldt and Dolly Loomans, University of Amsterdam; published in *Urban Studies*
- Publication date: 2024, volume 61, issue 7, pages 1393–1410 (first published online 29 November 2023)
- Original URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00420980231208624
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/00420980231208624
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

This paper examines the implementation of housing-led responses to homelessness in the Netherlands at a time of affordable-housing shortages, health-care austerity, decentralisation and deinstitutionalisation. The authors distinguish the broad category of housing-led responses from Housing First programmes that follow the full original model. Housing-led approaches make independent permanent housing the principal response to homelessness, contrasting with a staircase model in which people progress through shelters and transitional accommodation after demonstrating housing readiness.

The analysis draws on a larger longitudinal qualitative project conducted across seven Dutch regions and 34 municipalities between 2018 and 2022. The research comprised 1,045 interviews with 536 participants: 278 residents of homeless shelters or long-term care facilities and 258 professional stakeholders from housing associations, municipalities, care organisations, insurance companies and the police. Participants were interviewed between two and four times. This allowed the researchers to follow both individual routes towards independent housing and the changing relationships among housing, care and enforcement organisations.

Housing-led approaches were gaining support in all seven regions, including agreements to make social rented homes available to people leaving shelters and care institutions. The paper nevertheless finds that the supply shortage and policy emphasis on self-reliance restricted access. People could be judged too independent to qualify for priority housing but also too complex or insufficiently ready to manage a home. With no consistent national framework, eligibility and enforcement varied and could depend on the values and risk judgements of local actors.

Conditionality often continued after rehousing. Some participants received conditional or temporary leases tied to accepting support or meeting behavioural requirements. Housing associations, care providers and police became involved in monitoring compliance and deciding whether tenancies continued. The authors describe cases in which gaps in home-based support contributed to crisis or eviction, while police undertook roles for which health or care services were unavailable. Denial-of-service lists could prevent people evicted from social housing from returning to that sector, leaving shelters, institutions, rough sleeping or expensive private renting as the remaining options.

The authors interpret these arrangements through research on poverty management. Housing-led policy moves people from public space, shelters and institutions into private homes, but can also move practices of surveillance, discipline and punishment with them. A regular independent home is presented as a much-needed improvement, yet a conditional contract, the possibility of eviction and control by several institutions can undermine privacy, autonomy and the sense of security associated with home.

The paper concludes that the implementation of housing-led policy must be understood in its institutional setting. Affordable-housing scarcity, insufficient specialised support, decentralised responsibility and doctrines of self-sufficiency can preserve elements of the staircase model. Housing-led approaches are more humane and effective than that model, the authors argue, but are not automatically unconditional and must accompany policies addressing systemic poverty.

## Main arguments and findings

- Housing-led policy depends on coordination between housing and care, and its implementation is shaped by the supply of affordable and social housing.
- Housing shortages make self-reliance requirements unrealistic for people unable to access market housing.
- Eligibility rules can exclude people considered either too independent or too complex, preserving housing-readiness tests associated with the staircase model.
- Conditional leases may make housing and specialist support available, but can also weaken tenant rights and extend surveillance and discipline into the home.
- Housing associations and police increasingly perform screening, care and enforcement roles without necessarily having appropriate expertise.
- Gaps or delays in support after rehousing can contribute to crisis, tenancy breach, eviction and renewed homelessness.
- A dwelling can provide privacy, autonomy, belonging and security, but a permanent address alone does not guarantee those qualities.

## Qualifications and limitations

- The study concerns Dutch governance, welfare, housing and legal arrangements; its institutional findings should not be transferred unchanged to other countries.
- It is a qualitative longitudinal study rather than an experimental evaluation of Housing First or a measurement of programme effectiveness.
- The broader project was not designed solely around independent housing, so the interviews provide limited in-depth evidence about experiences of surveillance, conditionality and control in the home.
- Housing-led policy was relatively new during the research period, and most participants had held conditional tenancies for no more than three or four years.
- The authors use the broader term “housing-led” because the initiatives studied did not consistently follow all Housing First principles.
- The paper studies housing and service governance, not building methods or modular construction.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations.

- ExoSett addresses accommodation, not homelessness itself. It could not replace health and social care, fair allocation, secure tenancy arrangements, adequate incomes or wider policy.
- Housing supply is only one part of resolving homelessness, but housing-led approaches require an actual supply of suitable permanent homes. A building system may be relevant at this physical-infrastructure level.
- ExoSett may offer another way to create, adapt, refurbish and manage accommodation; it does not by itself make that accommodation affordable, well located or available to people who need it.
- Future social-housing or homelessness examples should treat a home as a place of privacy, security, autonomy and belonging, not as a unit into which a person is placed.
- Adaptability should be understood as a property of the building system. It must not make the resident’s home temporary, weaken tenure or legitimise routine relocation.
- Design and operational research should examine resident control, privacy during maintenance or module replacement, continuity of occupation and the separation of building management from care or tenancy enforcement.
