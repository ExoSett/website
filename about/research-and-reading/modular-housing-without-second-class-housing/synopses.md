# The Dos and Don’ts of Modular Housing

## Source

- Slug: `modular-housing-without-second-class-housing`
- Author / organisation: Ioana Vlad, Housing Policy Officer, FEANTSA; Housing Solutions Platform
- Publication date: March 2025
- Original URL: https://www.feantsa.org/files/Themes/HSP/2025/dos-and-donts/The_Dos__Donts_of_Modular_Housing.pdf
- Date accessed: 9 August 2026

## Synopsis

FEANTSA’s policy brief evaluates modular housing through housing rights and adequacy rather than through construction efficiency alone. It accepts that off-site and industrialised methods can produce high-quality, attractive and sustainable homes quickly, but argues that the same methods have repeatedly been used to provide inferior, segregated or insecure accommodation for people facing homelessness, displacement or discrimination. The brief’s question is therefore not whether modular technology is inherently good or bad, but under what policy, legal, financial and social conditions it is used.

The introduction uses container accommodation in Ealing and cases involving Roma communities in Italy and Romania to show how an emergency measure can become prolonged and harmful. Reported problems include cramped interiors, thermal discomfort, poor sanitation and services, isolation and indefinite “temporary” residence. The brief distinguishes converted shipping containers from purpose-built modular construction, but notes that terminology often blurs different products and that negative experiences can stigmatise the whole category.

It situates MMC within European and national policy. Governments promote industrialisation to increase construction productivity, shorten programmes, respond to labour shortages, reduce waste and emissions, and supply affordable or social housing. Examples from the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands and Barcelona show different uses, including permanent public housing, rapid-build programmes and flexible temporary housing. Delivery targets and claims of speed or savings have not always been realised, and high-quality modular housing can cost about as much as conventional construction. Speed and environmental performance may be more dependable advantages than very low price.

The analytical framework is the right to adequate housing. Habitability requires sufficient space, safety, thermal comfort, protection from weather and health hazards. Adequacy also includes water, sanitation, energy and other essential services; affordability; accessibility; a location connected to employment, education, healthcare and community; cultural adequacy; and security of tenure. The construction method does not justify lowering any of these standards. Temporary status is particularly risky where insufficient permanent social housing causes short placements to continue for years.

Several examples show modular projects operating outside or below ordinary social-housing protections. The brief describes parallel allocation rules, punitive eligibility conditions, forced relocation and segregated sites. Its interview with Eugen Ghiță of RomaJust gives a detailed account of Romanian Roma families placed in bare containers without adequate water or sanitation after evictions, with legal remedies delayed or ignored. These are presented as failures of rights, governance and discrimination, not merely technical defects.

Resident experience, language and aesthetics also matter. Research on Dublin rapid-build housing found that “modular” and “rapid build” could evoke post-war prefabs, temporary status and stigma even where technical quality matched conventional housing. Design, cladding and integration can reduce visible distinction, but terminology alone cannot overcome insecurity or poor treatment. Mixed communities and connections to ordinary neighbourhood life may help; segregated provision for a labelled vulnerable group can reinforce exclusion.

The recommendations call for modular homes to meet the same standards and legal protections as other homes; for residents to participate in design and evaluation; for temporary schemes to have credible routes to permanent housing; and for sites to be integrated, serviced and connected. Public authorities should avoid using modularity to create a second tier of housing. Adequate investment and monitoring are essential because manufacturing technology cannot compensate for inadequate land, tenure, services or policy.

## Main arguments and findings

- Modular construction is capable of good permanent housing but has also been used for inadequate and discriminatory emergency provision.
- Adequacy includes habitability, services, affordability, accessibility, location, cultural suitability and secure tenure.
- Temporary modular accommodation can become long-term when permanent social housing is unavailable.
- High quality still requires adequate investment; modular construction is not automatically much cheaper.
- Terminology, appearance and separation can stigmatise residents and associate tenure or homelessness with a visibly distinct housing type.
- Resident participation and evaluation should shape projects.
- Modular housing should remain within ordinary housing law and standards rather than creating parallel, weaker provision.

## Qualifications and limitations

- This is an advocacy-oriented policy synthesis, not a systematic review or comparative effectiveness study.
- It combines policy documents, academic studies, journalism, case reports and an interview, whose evidence bases differ.
- Severe container-housing abuses should not be generalised to all purpose-built modular housing; the brief itself distinguishes these forms.
- Examples span different countries, laws and welfare systems and are not directly interchangeable.
- Some speed, cost and sustainability figures are quoted from policy or industry sources rather than independently tested.
- Recommendations establish normative safeguards but do not provide a detailed delivery or costing model.

## Relevance to ExoSett

The following points are ExoSett interpretations.

- The brief strongly supports the principle that adaptability is a means and does not reduce what residents can expect from a home.
- It exposes risks that ExoSett could be used to create small, isolated, insecure or visibly second-tier accommodation despite good engineering.
- Separating structure, services, façades and accommodation may help buildings respond to context and improve over time, but cannot guarantee dignity, tenure or integration.
- ExoSett should develop explicit minimum expectations for space, comfort, services, accessibility, privacy, maintenance, location and resident involvement.
- Further research should examine governance safeguards, resident control during module replacement, anti-stigma design, and whether long-lived infrastructure actually supports permanent housing rather than prolonged temporary use.
